# Can I sell my share of an inherited house if my siblings won't sell?

Often, yes. When several relatives inherit a house together, each one usually owns an undivided share. In most cases a co-owner can sell their own share without the others signing. This guide explains how that works, when it doesn't, and what it means for the family members who keep their shares.

*This is general information, not legal advice. Every title is different; have a licensed Minnesota attorney confirm how this applies to your property.*

## First: what kind of share do you own?

Minnesota recognizes ownership by one person, in joint tenancy, and in common (Minn. Stat. § 500.19, subd. 1). For inherited houses, three situations come up most:

| What you own | What it means | Can you sell it on your own? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tenant in common** (common for heirs) | You own a percentage of the whole property, shared with the others. | Usually yes. The buyer steps into your share. |
| **Joint tenant** | Co-owners with a right of survivorship. | Usually yes, though selling generally changes the joint tenancy. Ask an attorney. |
| **Remainder in a homestead** (a parent has a life estate) | A surviving spouse may hold the right to live there for life; children hold the remainder (Minn. Stat. § 524.2-402(a)). | You can sell a remainder, but it doesn't come with the right to live there or rent it until the life estate ends, so it's worth much less than a share of the house. |

If probate or a decree of descent hasn't happened yet, your share may not be on record in your name. That usually has to be resolved first; see [inherited property](/inherited-property/).

## What happens when you sell your share

- **You're paid for your share** and you're no longer an owner.
- **The other owners keep their shares.** Nothing about their ownership changes.
- **The buyer becomes a co-owner** with the same rights you had, including a say in what happens to the property.

## If the co-owners still can't agree: partition

Any co-owner, including someone who bought a share, can ask a court to divide the property or order it sold and split the proceeds. This is called partition.

**Minnesota's Partition Act changed the rules for cases filed on or after August 1, 2025** (Minn. Stat. §§ 558A.01, 558A.02). Under the new act:

- A co-owner may ask for division, a sale, or "any other fair and equitable remedy" (§ 558A.03, subd. 1).
- Before deciding whether to divide the property, the court must weigh factors including how long the family has owned it, sentimental and ancestral attachment, how the property is being used, and who has paid the taxes, insurance and upkeep (§ 558A.11). No single factor decides it (§ 558A.11(b)).
- If the property is sold, the referee must sell it in a way designed to get the highest and best price (§ 558A.10), and the sale must be approved by the court (§ 558A.14).
- A co-owner may buy at the sale (§ 558A.15).

Partition is a lawsuit, with costs and time attached. Most families who co-own a property reach an agreement long before that point. Knowing the rules helps everyone negotiate.

## Questions to ask any buyer of your share

- What will you pay, and is it in writing?
- What do you plan to do with your share afterward?
- Will you talk with my relatives respectfully, and how?
- Who pays the title and closing costs?
- Can my attorney review the agreement first?

## Frequently asked questions

**Do my siblings have to sign?**
Usually not, for a tenant-in-common share. Every title is different, so we confirm it for your property before you decide.

**Will my siblings be notified?**
The sale is recorded publicly, and the new co-owner will be in touch with them. It's usually kinder to tell them yourself first.

**What is my share worth?**
Usually less than your percentage of the whole house's value, because a buyer of a share can't sell or use the whole property alone. A remainder interest behind a life estate is worth less still.

**Can a buyer force a sale of the whole house?**
Any co-owner can ask a court for partition, and since August 1, 2025 the court must weigh family history and attachment before deciding how (Minn. Stat. § 558A.11).
